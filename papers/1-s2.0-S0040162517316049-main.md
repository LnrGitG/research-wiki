#### 1. Introduction 

The paper addresses a problem that is closely connected with the forecasts of official statistical indicators produced monthly by national departments of statistics in all the European countries based on data gathered through sample surveys. For all these indicators, including the youth unemployment rate, it is useful to have forecasts that give some idea of the variations to be expected in the short term, not only because they can serve as preliminary estimates of the phenomenon examined but also because they can be incorporated into econometric models to provide indications of the prospects for the economic system as a whole. The Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT) produces such forecasts by means of statistical models that make no use of information from external sources and rely only on the data provided by its own sample surveys. 

The purpose of the experiment presented in this paper is to ascertain if the combined use of information from Google Trends (GT) and the ISTAT labour force survey makes any improvement to the forecasting of the youth unemployment rate in terms of the accuracy and timeliness of the estimates. 

Many scholars and researchers have become aware in recent years that the vast amount of information to be derived from the mass of online search data available could prove useful in the study of social 

phenomena (Askitas and Zimmermann, 2015; Chamberlin, 2010; Choi and Varian, 2012; Daas et al., 2015; Einav and Levin, 2014; Guzman, 2011; Marchetti et al., 2015). This is because the information that people disclose about their needs using the Internet (Ettredge et al., 2005) can shed light on the variability of numerous phenomena under examination. A review of the application of these types of data in different fields can be found in Hassani and Silva (2015). 

With particular reference to Google Trends data, it is easy to state that the relevance and potential of this tool lies in their characteristic of containing a huge amount of information that is easily accessible yet difficult to obtain in another way. This represents a great opportunity especially for statistical purposes, and the interest of the scientific community is evident by the amount of work proposed in literature over the past 10 years. Only a few of the most recent studies are involved in small area estimation problems (Porter et al., 2013; Rao and Molina, 2015, p. 159–170) in the spread of infectious diseases (Anggraeni and Aristiani, 2017; Teng et al., 2017), in numerous economic contexts such as consumer purchases (Schmidt and Vosen, 2013), housing market (Limnios and You, 2016), tourist inflows (Dinis et al., 2017), forecasting energy (Hassani and Silva, 2016), credit developments (Burdeau and Knitzler, 2017), and social phenomena (Nghiem et al., 2016; Nixon, 2016; Parker et al., 2017).

Regarding the phenomenon studied in this work, numerous authors suggest the use of Internet data to forecast unemployment (Anvik and Gjelstad, 2010; Askitas and Zimmermann, 2009; D'Amuri and Marcucci, 2017; Falorsi et al., 2015; McLaren and Shanbhogue, 2011), and the results show that they can indeed be regarded as useful in the estimation procedure. 

The monthly estimates of Italian employment and unemployment published by the ISTAT have been accompanied regularly in the mass media by a particular focus on youth unemployment, the levels of which rose steadily during the recent economic crisis and are markedly above the European average, hence the interest in providing more timely and reliable information regarding this aspect. 

Moreover, the official estimate of the youth unemployment rate in Italy is based on a monthly sample survey and generally becomes available about 30 days after the end of the month in question due to the time required to gather and process the relevant data. It could prove to be useful in many situations to have reliable indications of the results of an ongoing survey before all of its phases have been completed. In other words, the need may arise for a preview or nowcast (Choi and Varian, 2012) of the youth unemployment rate for which the survey is being carried out in the same period. The problem therefore is to produce estimates that are available while the actual survey is still under way – or in any case in a short space of time – to provide reliable information on the indicator in the current month and its variation with respect to the previous one (Ayoubkhani, 2012; D'Alò et al., 2006). 

It should be noted that for Italy, as well as the other European countries, the information required by the European Community for purposes of economic analysis is vast and drawn from a variety of largescale surveys based both on samples and on censuses. These requirements also are laid down in the European Community regulations on short-term business statistics in force since August 2005. The same date saw the launch of the Action Plan on Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) Statistical Requirements by Eurostat and the Central European Bank with the involvement of the European national departments of statistics in an effort to reduce the time required for the production and circulation of the most important indicators that are essential to the short-term analysis of the European economy. 

The purpose of this work is to ascertain if the use of information from Google Trends together with data from the time series of the ISTAT labour force survey can improve the accuracy and timeliness of the youth unemployment rate forecast (Barreira et al., 2013; Fondeur and Karamè, 2013) and make it possible to provide an “immediate” forecast for use, even if only as a pointer, while awaiting the official figures. 

Google Trends information is subjected to no form of quality control and, in our opinion, can be used solely as a sort of “snapshot”, providing indications about the phenomenon of interest and its evolution in time and space. As C.F. Citro (2014) observes: 

Statistical agencies need, above all, sources of data that cover a known population with error properties that are reasonably well understood and that are not likely to change under their feet – characteristics that are not inherent in such data sources as autonomous interactions with websites on the Internet. There are, however, at least two ways in which household survey-based statistical agency programs could obtain an “edge” from non-traditional sources: one is to improve timeliness for preliminary estimates of key statistics; and the other is to provide leading indicators of social change (e.g., the emergence of new occupations and fields of training) that alert statistical agencies to needed changes in their concepts and measures. 

Goel et al. (2010) provide a useful survey of the work undertaken in this area and describe some of the limitations of web search data. As they point out, such data are readily obtainable and often helpful in making forecasts but may not provide dramatic increases in predictability. 

As the use of the Internet for job seeking is more widespread among the younger population, the trend in the monthly number of job searches made through Google can provide useful information for forecasting the youth unemployment rate. The basic idea is that an increase in this rate is accompanied by an increase in the number of young people seeking employment opportunities through online searches. As shown in Section 2.1, this idea is borne out by the results of a specific query in ISTAT's monthly survey on the labour force. 

Google Trends data corresponding to the keyword “offerte di lavoro” (job offers) are used in this paper as auxiliary information to forecast the Italian youth unemployment rate. They belong to the sphere of what is known in the literature as “big data” (Ceron et al., 2014; Choi and Varian, 2012; Einav and Levin, 2014), which have the characteristic of being immediately available and capable of supplying an up-to-date picture of social and economic phenomena. As Chamberlin (2010) writes: 

As this search data from Google Trends is available in real time, any significant relationship could be potentially exploited for nowcasting. Google Trends data may also be used informally to pick up on changing patterns and rising trends in search queries and the implications they have for types of economic activity and spending. 

Moreover, Google Trends data generally can be used at a very low cost, which constitutes a further reason for the interest in the sphere of official statistical surveys. 

However, there are some aspects in the use of GT data processing that cannot be left out. Among these, one of the most important is the choice of keywords to be used for the selection of data sets. Different keywords lead to different results; however, if the selection criterion derives from an adequate knowledge of the phenomenon under study and if you carry out controls on a large number of keywords and their combinations, you can obtain useful results. 

In extracting information from large amounts of data, the errors that can be made are multiple (Braaksma and Zeelemberg, 2015; Nuti et al., 2014; Zeelemberg and Braaksma, 2017, p. 274–296) and many are the consequences of an inappropriate choice of keywords. The most probable error, especially in the event that GT information is used to make forecasts by means of regression models – as is the case in this paper – is to run into spurious correlations. The GT series generally is extracted on the basis of the correlation that it shows with the phenomenon under study. If two or more phenomena are statistically correlated, however, it does not necessarily mean that there is a direct cause-effect link between them because this correlation can be completely random (i.e. spurious) or dependent on a further variable in common. 

To reduce this risk, different keywords or combinations of keywords are chosen, the variability of the series extracted from Google Trends is studied as keywords inserted in the query shares change, and finally for the extracted series the statistical significance of the correlation between these and the variable being studied is verified. 

To evaluate the utility of GT data in forecasting the Italian youth unemployment rate, a comparison was developed between results obtained by means of an ARIMA model (Box et al., 2007), making no use of auxiliary GT information, and a vector autoregressive (VAR) model (Lutkephol, 2007), harnessing the correlation of official and Internet search data. 

The ARIMA methodology is the one already employed by the ISTAT to obtain forecasts in the sphere of national accounts and indicators of trends in the Italian economy. These forecasts are always short term (no longer than 3 months) and provide information on the expected tendency of the economic indicator in a given month of the year with respect to its value as published based on survey data 2 or 3 months earlier. In other words, the purpose of the forecasts is to provide

#### 2. Material and methods 

The phenomenon of youth unemployment in Italy, its scale, and the connections with Internet searches for job opportunities are briefly outlined in Section 2.1. The time series used for the estimation of the forecasting models is the monthly ISTAT series on the youth unemployment rate (YUR) in Italy and the monthly Google Trends (GT) series for the keyword “offerte di lavoro” (job offers). The latter refers to the number of searches made per month on Google specifying the words offerte di lavoro. Both series are described in detail in Section 2.2. 

It should be noted that the use of this keyword is justified on the grounds of its popularity among competing job search-related keywords, as can be ascertained by comparing its relative incidence with that of other search terms (D'Amuri and Marcucci, 2017). Furthermore, its broad definition makes it ideally robust to irrelevant strong variations connected to the demand or supply for specific subgroups of job seekers. Although the fact that not all workers have access to the Internet also must be taken into account, this is a minor issue in view of its ever-increasing use as a way of seeking employment, especially by the young. Moreover, the two monthly series of the LFS regarding young people − the overall sample and the subsample of online job seekers − show marked similarities. 

It should be noted that many other sources of the big data type are available to provide information on unemployment, including the number of positions posted by employers on their own websites or with Internet-based companies that mediate between employers and employees. Although these provide useful information with respect to categories of workers or geographical areas (Larsen et al., 2015), GT presents many characteristics that make it preferable to other sources in relation to the problem addressed here. 

First and foremost, GT supplies information in the form of time series, which is obviously necessary to produce forecasts by means of the statistical models employed. Moreover, it is a source of homogeneous information that is available on one platform and provides information produced based on only one criterion. In other words, there is no need for procedures to merge information from different online “containers”, which generally is a very expensive and time-consuming process that also involves the risk of aggregating the data incorrectly. Finally, Google is a search engine that is widely used for job seeking in Italy, where there are no public or private employment agencies to bring the supply and demand for work together on a large scale throughout the nation and for a broad range of jobs and professions. 

In any case, attention should be drawn to some limitations of GT. It does not distinguish between those seeking jobs because they have lost theirs and those who simply would like a change. It provides no data on the gender and age of the online job seekers, and multiple searches by the same individual are counted separately. In other words, GT captures only the temporal trend of the number of those seeking jobs online with no reference to their reasons for doing so or their individual characteristics. 

As the monthly ISTAT labour force survey indicates a marked increase, especially among the young, in the percentage of unemployed 

persons seeking jobs online (see Section 2.1), the choice of GT as a source appears to be reasonable, not least as the sole purpose of this work is to provide a forecast of the youth unemployment rate. 

The forecasting models used are those typically employed in the analysis of time series: a VAR model using both official and online information and an ARIMA model estimated solely based on official data. A number of considerations regarding the choice of models are subsequently developed in Section 2.3. 

#### 2.1. Youth unemployment in Italy 

The youth unemployment rate is defined at the European level as – the ratio of unemployed persons age 15 24 to the corresponding labour force (employed and unemployed). It therefore represents the proportion of young unemployed people among those who are active in the labour market. The calculation of the unemployment rate thus excludes young people who are inactive, understood as those who are jobless but not looking or available for work, in most cases because they are still studying (inactive people account for more than 70% of the population in this age group). 

The youth unemployment rate thus defined almost doubled during the recent economic crisis, rising from about 20% in 2007 to more than 40% between 2013 and 2015. Although recent months have shown a slight change in direction, the rate still is close to maximum levels, 39.1% being the latest estimate for the month of February 2016 (ISTAT, 2015a). 

In addition to this marked growth, the youth unemployment rate is far higher in Italy than in the other EU countries – 42.7% in 2014 as opposed to the EU average of 22.2%. This makes the youth unemployment rate in Italy the fourth highest of the 28 member countries, preceded only by Spain, Greece, and Croatia. The position is slightly better (seventh place) with regard to the total unemployment rate: 12.7% in Italy versus the EU average of 10.2%. These few figures show that the difference between Italy and the EU is considerably greater concerning the difficulties encountered by young people in the labour market. 

As Mario Draghi, president of the European Central Bank, points out in a recent interview (The Guardian, 11 March 2016), analysts also are concerned about the medium and long-term consequences of young people's failure to participate in the labour market in terms of the loss of human capital. According to the theory of human capital, productivity on the labour market depends on various factors, among which, apart from individual abilities and the skills acquired through education and training courses, importance is also attached to what is learned on the job. As time passes, the knowledge acquired tends to become obsolete or is lost through disuse. Apart from the obvious economic and social difficulties involved, unemployment therefore also leads to the loss of human capital, affecting the future working life of the young people concerned. These elements help explain why the estimation of the youth unemployment rate is a matter of great interest at both the Italian and the European level. 

The data gathered in the ISTAT labour force survey reveal considerable use of the Internet in employment seeking, especially among the young. The data related to the specific query introduced in the survey show that, with respect to the total number of people seeking work, the proportion of those using the Internet for this purpose rose from 20% in January 2004 to 60% in December 2014 (Fig. 1). 

As is to be expected, given the greater use of computers and the Internet by the younger generations, these figures are higher for the sphere of youth unemployment, with a rise from approximately 25% in January 2004 to approximately 70% in December 2014. This makes it reasonable to suggest that the trend of the monthly offerte di lavoro (job offers) query share constitutes a useful tool for forecasting the youth unemployment rate.

#### 2.3. The model 

Two types of time series models were estimated to ascertain if Google Trends data can be of use in forecasting the youth unemployment rate. One is an ARIMA model, using only the information of the official series for the youth unemployment rate, while the other − a VAR model using cross-correlation of the two series (Johansen, 1995) − combines the official series for the youth unemployment rate with the series of the job offers query share. It should be pointed out that in neither case is this paper concerned with putting forward a model to identify the determinants of the variability of the youth unemployment rate in Italy. It also should be noted that the ARIMA model, estimated solely on the official ISTAT series, is presented only as a benchmark. The sole purpose of the estimation of a very simple time series model is to show if, with respect to the use of the official series alone, the introduction of Internet search data can prove useful with a view to forecasting. 

The use of the ARIMAX model (Hamilton, 1994) with GT as an exogenous variable is widely advocated in the recent literature on the use of big data to forecast economic indicators (D'Amuri and Marcucci, 2017; Vicente et al., 2015). In our view, however, this type of model is not appropriate in this case for at least three reasons. First, regarding the GT variable as exogenous appears to be questionable. Both the GT and YUR series are expressions of the same phenomenon, and the latter cannot be regarded as causing the former. Second, the temporal dynamics of the auxiliary series (GT) is not taken into account in an ARIMAX model. It instead is our opinion that the characteristics with which the GT series evolves over time must be considered explicitly. Its temporal dynamics is unquestionably connected with that of the youth unemployment rate to be forecasted. This does not happen in a model of the ARIMAX type, in which the GT variable, whether contemporary or delayed, is regarded as deterministic. The third reason is that ARIMAX models are not suitable for forecasting more than one step ahead unless introducing an arbitrary ad hoc delay for the GT variable linked to the forecast horizon. 

The VAR model presented here, other than considering explicitly GT and YUR variables as endogenous, takes into account the temporal dynamics of both series as well as their reciprocal effects. Contrary to what happens with the ARIMAX model, the VAR model makes the simultaneous forecasting of both series possible more than one step ahead. 

For this purpose an out-of-sample procedure (Tsay, 2010, pp. 215–216) was performed by setting horizons of 1 month and 3 months (h = 1, h = 3); as a result, one and three observations available were excluded from the estimation of the model. 

The choice of the time horizons is related to the two aims pursued: to obtain preliminary monthly estimates capable of giving an indication of the expected variations in the phenomenon of interest from one month to the next prior to the completion of the official sample survey and to obtain reliable forecasts of the youth unemployment rate that can be used in the quarterly forecast models of the Italian economic system (ISTAT, 2015b). It should be noted in this connection that the high degree of intra-annual variability of unemployment in general and therefore also of Italian youth unemployment makes the use of broader time horizons inadvisable.

#### 3. Results and discussion 

#### 3.1. Models' estimation 

It is necessary when working with models for time series to evaluate the nature of the series before estimating the model itself: for each of the series the stationarity hypothesis (Hamilton, 1994, p. 45) is verified and, in case the series want to be studied together, it is necessary to verify if they present common stochastic trends. 

The Johansen test (Johansen, 1991) that allows both these evaluations to be carried out was calculated on the YUR and GT series indicated, respectively, with y1t and y2t. 

This is a sequential test: it occurs initially if the series are only integrated (null hypothesis) or integrated and linked by a co-integration relationship (alternative hypothesis). In the second test, which is carried out only if the null hypothesis of the previous one is rejected, we compare the hypothesis that they are co-integrated against the hypothesis that they are stationary. 

Since the value of the test statistic (JS) for the first problem is smaller than the 95th percentile of Johansen's distribution<sup>1</sup> (Table 1), we conclude that the series are integrated of order one (null hypothesis) and therefore do not proceed with the second test. Since the series are integrated they are non-stationary; to solve the problem of non-stationarity, we use the first differences of the series instead of the original ones. Finally, since the series are not co-integrated, the VAR model is the most suitable to represent the joint variability of the two series. 

The results of Johansen's test about the significance of the two series' joint variability also enable us to conclude that their relationship cannot be supposed spurious, and therefore we can reasonably consider the choice of the keywords offerte di lavoro to be appropriate.

#### 4. Conclusions 

In Italy, as in the other European countries, analysts of the labour market pay great attention to studying the trend of unemployment in general and youth unemployment in particular, above all from a shortterm perspective. It therefore is important to provide indications of the trends in these aggregates that are as accurate and reliable as possible to permit correct analysis of the national situation and use of the data for effective policymaking by the relevant authorities. Hence, the purpose of this paper was to ascertain the possibility of improving the forecasts of youth unemployment in Italy by combining the official sources with big data. 

Such forecasts are of use to the ISTAT for two reasons. First, they constitute advance estimates of the phenomenon under examination and can be used for preliminary analysis before the official estimates have been produced. Second, they can be used within the framework of macro-econometric forecasting models to provide indications of the prospects for the economic system as a whole. 

The results confirm the close connection between the level of youth unemployment in Italy and the use of the Internet to look for work, so this method can be used as auxiliary information in forecasting, at least for short and medium forecast horizons. 

The VAR model, characterized by the use of the GT series, produces better results in terms of forecast error than the ARIMA, which is based exclusively on information from the official series for the youth unemployment rate. This suggests that a snapshot of what is happening in society, although not accurate in terms of statistical quality, provides readily available, low-cost information that can be used to draw at least some preliminary conclusions about an indicator of great social, political, and economic importance. This confirms the initial idea that what can be exploited in terms of forecasting capacity of the GT series is the way in which it moves in unison with the youth unemployment rate series and thus reflects the changes in the population manifested through its use of the Internet. 

In the light of these considerations, it appears to be possible to conclude that the use of GT data as an auxiliary variable – together with a suitable forecasting model – is a good way to obtain reliable forecasts of the unemployment rate. At the same time, however, the fact cannot be overlooked that the level of forecast error is by no means negligible. In other words, although the results obtained through the use of GT data are encouraging and suggest that we should continue in this direction, the procedures for extracting information from GT and the possible application in other areas of estimation require further investigation. 

It should be pointed out that, given the nature of the GT and the YUR data, the procedure put forward here is replicable for small-scale contexts, such as particular categories of labour and/or specific territorial and temporal spheres. Also, the model proposed here was applied to the unemployment rate series in Italy, which presents situations of marked variability, making it difficult to isolate the error component effectively from the structural one due to the economic crisis that has struck Italy harder than other European countries in the last few years. 

Finally, a general remark seems to us to be necessary. The use of Google Trends in official statistics as an aid to statistical production, or for the production of additional information, is a great opportunity and a challenge for the national statistical institutes, but it implies the need for a careful control of the quality of information. Although the debate often is focused on the issues of information volume, the velocity at which it can be acquired, and its variety, national statistical offices also

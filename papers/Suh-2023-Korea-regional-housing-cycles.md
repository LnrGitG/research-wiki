#### **1. Introduction** 

Housing cycles are an important source of macroeconomic fluctuations. For example, Leamer (2007) suggests that residential investment is the best early warning signal of future recessions. Iacoviello (2005), Iacoviello and Neri (2010) suggest that the collateral channel associated with housing goods helps explain business cycle dynamics, and spillovers from housing markets exist and have become stronger over time. 

Housing cycle variations may exist across regions in an economy large enough that it cannot be characterized as a single housing market. Panel (a) of Fig. 1 displays the housing price indices of two regions in South Korea (hereafter, Korea): the Seoul Metropolitan Area and the Southeast Area.<sup>2</sup> The figure suggests the existence of regionally heterogeneous housing cycles in Korea, highlighted by two conspicuous periods that lasted for three to five years. From 2005 to 2008, the housing price index in the Seoul Metropolitan Area surged, while housing markets in the Southeast Area only mildly appreciated. Conversely, from 2010 to 2015, housing prices in the Southeast Area rose rapidly, while housing markets in the Seoul Metropolitan Area experienced a significant slump. 

Various factors can cause these regionally heterogeneous housing cycles. Housing demand is affected by household income; thus, regional macroeconomic conditions causing household income to fluctuate differently across regions can be a source of regionally heterogeneous housing cycles. Another factor is changes in government policies that affect regional housing markets, such as large-scale land developments, which can influence regional housing supply. Additionally, shocks in housing-related taxes and mortgage lending regulations imposed with macroprudential purposes may vary across regions. 

Panel (b) of Fig. 1 indicates that regional housing cycles can be independent of regional macroeconomic cycles. From 2005 to 2015, although the macroeconomic cycles in the two regions were similar, housing cycles varied notably. This variation implies that other than regional macroeconomic conditions, idiosyncratic shocks originating from regional housing markets can be a source of regional housing cycles. Moreover, these idiosyncratic shocks can influence regional macroeconomic variables, such as residential investment and consumption, and eventually have welfare implications for economic agents.

Furthermore, an increase in household debt usually accompanies the housing boom. As household wealth and debt increase in the region, nontrivial implications may arise for welfare inequality between the agents that experience a housing boom and those in other regions. This problem can be even more complicated if a low housing supply in the region primarily or partially causes the increases in housing prices, indicating there are fewer housing goods to consume, affecting welfare negatively, and offsetting the positive contribution from the wealth effect. Moreover, governments can implement stabilization policies to alleviate overheating in regional housing markets, prevent the formation of housing or credit bubbles, and reduce regional inequality. 

In this respect, this study primarily investigates the macroeconomic implications of the regionally heterogeneous cycles caused by idiosyncratic shocks in regional housing markets, such as Korea experienced. Specifically, we address the following questions. How do regional housing shocks affect regional output, consumption, and welfare? What are the effects of government policies such as tax, housing supply, macroprudential, and monetary policies in response to these shocks? 

This study analyzes these questions by employing a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) model that features two regions across which consumption goods are tradable, but housing goods are attached to the regional markets. Agents in one region can invest in housing in the other region but cannot reside there. The model has other standard features in the New Keynesian literature: nominal rigidity, capital adjustment, and utilization costs. These features help the model fit data better and are useful for analyzing the effect of monetary policy on regional housing cycles. Model parameters are estimated using the Bayesian method and Korean data. Idiosyncratic shocks in regional housing supply or demand generate heterogeneous housing cycles. Specifically, we conduct a counterfactual experiment in which a series of idiosyncratic housing supply or demand shocks increases the housing price of one region by 15 percent. We analyze the response of the macroeconomic variables in both regions and how they are affected by the different use of policy instruments. 

Simulating regional housing cycles suggests that they can affect regional housing production, debt, and consumption. When a housing 

boom in region 1 is driven by a housing exuberance shock that represents the shock in housing demand, the region experiences a surge in housing production and an increase in consumption. The latter is mainly caused by the relaxation of borrowing constraints rather than the wealth effect. The shock can also cause inequality between regions by affecting welfare asymmetrically. The welfare of region 1 borrowers increases. Larger housing stocks provide more housing services, and borrowers receive additional welfare gains as they can use housing as collateral. In contrast, the welfare of all region 2 agents decreases. When region 1 housing prices increase via adverse housing supply shocks, this lowers the welfare of all region 1 agents. Decreased housing supply and housing services directly harm these agents. Conversely, the welfare of all region 2 agents increases. 

The Korean government adopted region-specific housing market stabilization measures, such as property tax, countercyclical loan-to-value (LTV), and housing supply policies. The simulation suggests that those policies can mitigate regional housing cycles; however, it takes large policy responses if a housing demand shock causes the cycle. The LTV policy has a relatively weak impact on housing prices, as it only applies to new loans. Expansionary housing supply policy increases region 1 housing production, but demand policies, such as tax and LTV policies, slightly reduce it. Given supply or demand shocks, the welfare of region 1 agents consistently improves under the housing supply policy that provides more housing services to them. Additionally, the LTV policy consistently reduces the welfare of region 1 borrowers by tightening their credit constraints. ‘‘Leaning against the wind’’ monetary policy is relatively ineffective in stabilizing regional housing prices, as its effect is dispersed to housing markets in other regions and consumption. Higher interest rates during housing price appreciations negatively affect the welfare of borrowers in all regions. 

#### _1.1. Literature review_ 

Previous studies confirm that housing cycles can vary across regions. Fratantoni and Schuh (2003) suggest that supply and demand in American regional housing markets may depend heavily on idiosyncratic and regional factors, providing evidence of regional heterogeneity in housing appreciation in the 1980s and 1990s. Del Negro and Otrok (2007) estimate a dynamic factor model on state-level housing price data and find that the local component mainly drove historical

housing price movements. A similar finding is reported by Fairchild et al. (2015), who estimate a dynamic factor model using the price/rent ratios of 23 major housing markets and find that local factors drive a large proportion of housing market volatility. 

Several studies have highlighted the role of housing markets in macroeconomic fluctuations. Davis and Heathcote (2005) develop a DSGE model that incorporates the housing sector to match housing market dynamics with the business cycle patterns. Iacoviello (2005), Iacoviello and Neri (2010) use housing DSGE models to examine the propagation effect of housing markets on the macroeconomy. This study’s model has some similar features to Iacoviello and Neri (2010), but we extend the model to include a multiregional setup with heterogeneous housing cycles and inter-regional housing investment. We also allow news shocks in housing markets to be sources of housing market fluctuations, as in Gomes and Mendicino (2015) and Ng and Feng (2016), who find a significant contribution of housing news shocks to the housing market and macroeconomic fluctuations. Moreover, we model household debt as a long-term amortizing mortgage loan, as in Alpanda and Zubairy (2017). Furthermore, this study has similarities to Lee and Song (2015) in estimating a housing DSGE model with Korean data and to Franz (2020) in discussing housing and borrower-related macroprudential policy tools in Korea. 

This study aims to understand how regionally heterogeneous housing cycles impact the regional economy and welfare and how government policies affect those cycles. In this sense, the closest work in the literature is Funke et al. (2021), which examines the two-region DSGE model with regional housing markets. They conclude that the region-specific LTV policy performs better than the leaning against the wind monetary policy or national LTV policy in stabilizing the housing price variance. Although the model frameworks are similar, we focus on how regional housing cycles affect each agent in each region differently rather than the aggregate measures they study. Furthermore, this current study considers both housing supply and demand shocks. It compares different measures of region-specific stabilization policies, including the housing supply policy that has not been frequently investigated in the literature, while Funke et al. (2021) only analyzes the housing preference shock and region-specific LTV policy. Furthermore, this study is close to Basto et al. (2019) in studying the impact of LTV policy. 

#### **2. Housing market stabilization policies in Korea** 

When governments face regionally heterogeneous housing cycles, they can implement stabilization policies to alleviate overheating in regional housing markets, prevent the formation of speculative or credit-driven housing bubbles, and reduce regional inequality. This section summarizes policy measures for housing market stabilization used by the Korean government in recent years. Following Shin and Yi (2019), these measures are classified into tax, macroprudential, and housing supply policies. These policy instruments also have a commonality, as they can be applied region-specifically, notably in regions that experience a housing boom. Specifically, the Korean government designated policy target regions, distinguished by Speculation, Speculative Overheating, and Adjustment-required zones, depending on the degree of overheating. Each zone is subject to different degrees of tax and macroprudential regulations, while regulations on Speculation Zones are the strongest. Zone designations are lifted when the government determines that housing markets have stabilized. Fig. 2 shows the time-varying number of policy target zones in the Seoul Metropolitan Area. The figure shows the countercyclical nature of the policies, as the number of target zones increases during the 2016–2021 housing appreciation period but decreases in 2022 as housing prices start to fall. Also, Jung and Lee (2017) identify 15 changes in LTV limits in Korea during 2002–2014, approximately one change per year. Those changes are countercyclical; LTV limits are lowered during housing booms and vice versa during housing market downturns. 

The following summarizes the details of each policy category.<sup>3</sup> First, for housing market stabilization purposes, the Korean government introduced the Comprehensive Property Tax in 2005, levied on the owners of multi-housing and high-priced housing units in addition to regular property tax. The tax rate is progressive depending on the property value. In July 2020, the Korean government raised the Comprehensive Property Tax rate from 0.6–3.2 percent to 1.2–6.0 percent in response to sharp housing price appreciations. In addition, the capital gains tax rate for multi-housing owners increased by 20–30 percent. 

Second, since 2002, the Korean government has implemented macroprudential regulations, such as LTV and debt-to-income (DTI) caps. Those macroprudential regulations are conducted countercyclically, leaning against the wind. Furthermore, different caps are applied across different policy target regions.<sup>4</sup> For example, Table 1 summarizes region-specific LTV ratios as of May 2018. The lowest caps are applied in Speculation Zones, and regulations are less restrictive in Speculative Overheating and Adjustment-required zones. In 2019, the government further strengthened LTV regulations. For Speculative Overheating Zones, the LTV cap was lowered to 40 percent for housing units valued up to 900 million Korean won (KRW), then 20 percent for the proportion of the housing value exceeding 900 million KRW. Moreover, mortgage borrowing is even banned for houses over 1.5 billion KRW. 

Third, the Korean government has implemented large-scale housing development plans and deregulated urban housing reconstruction procedures to boost the housing supply against rising housing prices. For example, in 2015, the government relaxed regulations on the minimum eligibility period for reconstruction, extending the grace period for three years to redeem excessive earnings from reconstruction, aiming to expedite the reconstruction of old housing to provide new housing. Furthermore, the government announced a long-term supply plan for 1.3 million units in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (15.2 percent of housing in the area as of 2019) in August 2020, followed by an additional 836,000 housing supply plan in February 2021. The plan includes the development of new cities around Seoul, mainly by lifting regulations on nearby urban greenbelt areas that were kept undeveloped for environmental preservation purposes. 

#### **3. Model** 

The key elements of the model are close to the one-country housing DSGE model proposed by Iacoviello and Neri (2010) and the tworegion housing DSGE model by Funke et al. (2021). The two regions are indexed by _𝑗_ = 1 _,_ 2. Consumption goods are assumed to be tradable between the regions; thus, a single price applies (i.e., goods inflation is the same in both regions). In contrast, housing goods and labor are attached to the regional markets<sup>5</sup> ; thus, housing prices and wages might differ between regions. 

Inter-regional housing investment is allowed; agents in one (‘‘home’’) region can purchase housing units in the other (‘‘external’’) region. In reality, inter-regional housing investment accounts for a significant proportion of housing purchases. The Korean Homeownership Statistics in 2019 suggests that agents outside the region own approximately 14 percent of housing. Although these inter-regional housing investors cannot reside in their external housing units, they receive rental fees from them. Moreover, these inter-regional housing assets can be pledged as collateral for borrowing. 

Both regions have identical population and economic structures, except for regionally idiosyncratic housing shocks, implying that both regions have a symmetric steady state in which the inter-regional trade balance is zero.

#### _3.3. Nominal rigidity_ 

Nominal rigidity exists in the price determination of consumption goods and wages. The form of price and wage rigidity in our model largely follows (Gerali et al., 2010), with slight modifications. Intermediate goods are purchased by retailers who produce differentiated products in a monopolistic competitive environment. Those differentiated goods are packaged for use in the final consumption of households. Retailers can optimally choose the price of their product given its demand schedule, but they face a quadratic cost in adjusting the price à la (Rotemberg, 1982). In Appendix A.2, we provide formal descriptions of this nominal rigidity.

We calibrate and estimate the model parameters using macroeconomic time-series data in Korea. This section discusses the details of the calibration and estimation procedures. 

#### _4.1. Calibration_ 

Some of the model parameters are calibrated. We use the calibration values of Iacoviello and Neri (2010) for the savers’ discount factor, depreciation rates, and the share of capital, land, and intermediate goods in housing production. Considering the lower risk premium during the sample period, we set the borrowers’ discount factor at 0.98, higher than the 0.97 of Iacoviello and Neri (2010). _𝜖_ , the habit formation parameter, and _𝛼_ , the share of saving households in the production function, are calibrated at 0.7 and 0.8, respectively. These values are consistent with the posterior mean values of Iacoviello and Neri (2010). The values of _𝜇𝑐_ (the capital share in consumption goods production) and _𝐽_ (the relative importance of housing services in household preferences) are chosen to match the sample consumption/GDP ratio (0.56) and residential investment/GDP ratio (0.04) in Korea. We also calibrate the government spending/GDP ratio to match the sample value of 0.158. 

The steady-state LTV ratio is set to 0.8. The home equity withdrawal rate _𝛾𝑚_ and debt amortization rate _𝜅𝑚_ are chosen to be close to the calibrated values presented by Alpanda and Zubairy (2017). _𝐽_<sup>_𝑅_</sup> , the relative preference for rental housing, is set to 0.0083. This value implies that outside agents own approximately 14 percent of housing in each region, matching the value of the Korean Homeownership Statistics in 2019. We choose a small value of 0.01 for _𝜙_<sup>_𝑅_</sup> , the sensitivity of the regional risk premium to inter-regional debt, so that inter-regional debt is negligible in the regional interest rate. _𝜏𝑠𝑠_<sup>_ℎ_issetto0.0025,implying</sup> that the steady-state property tax rate is approximately 1 percent when annualized. #### _4.2. Estimation_ 

The remaining model parameters are estimated using the Bayesian method. Because regional income accounting data are not available at a quarterly frequency, the estimation is conducted using national data. This strategy also fits our analysis because we assume both regions have identical economic structures, except for regionally idiosyncratic housing shocks. This approach means the structural parameters in both regions are the same. 

We use nine observable variables from Korean data: consumption, residential investment, nonresidential investment, government spending, the GDP deflator, labor hours in the goods sector, the threemonth certificate of deposit interest rate, the wage, and the housing price index. Among these, all the variables except the interest rate are log-differenced. As in Iacoviello and Neri (2010), consumption, investments, and government spending are normalized to per capita terms. The GDP deflator translates the nominal wage and housing price index into real values. We allow nonresidential investment and housing prices to have different trends from GDP growth. 

The data source for national income statistics and interest rates is the Bank of Korea Economic Statistics System, while wage and labor hours are from the Ministry of Employment and Labor. The housing price index is obtained from the Korea Real Estate Board, and the sample period is 2000:1Q–2019:3Q. 

Tables A.2 and A.3 in the Appendix report the prior distribution, posterior mean, and 90 percent highest posterior density interval obtained from the Markov Chain Monte Carlo draws. The prior distributions for the structural parameters and parameters governing the shock processes are mostly chosen to be consistent with those assumed by Iacoviello and Neri (2010). The parameters for capital, price, and wage adjustment costs have a gamma prior with a mean of 10 and a standard deviation of 0.25. The priors for the inverse Frisch elasticity of labor _𝜂_ and inverse elasticity of sectoral labor substitution _𝜓_ are a gamma distribution and normal distribution, respectively. Following the standard approach in the literature, the priors for the AR(1) coefficients and shock volatility of the exogenous processes have a beta distribution and an inverse gamma distribution, respectively. 

We linearize the model equations around the steady state to obtain the posterior distribution and simulate 300,000 sets of the posterior parameters using the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm. Excluding the burn-in period, the last 180,000 draws are used to formulate the posterior distributions. Geweke (1992)’s convergence statistics support the convergence of the posterior distribution of all parameters. 

Table A.4 (a) in the Appendix compares the key moments implied by the estimated model with the moments of sample data. Generally, the model exhibits higher volatility than the sample, but the difference is insignificant. Furthermore, the model closely follows the relative volatility pattern in the sample data, for example, high volatility in residential and nonresidential investments and low volatility in consumption. In addition, the model generates similar correlation patterns between the key variables to those in the sample data. For example, consumption positively correlates with nonresidential investment, wages, and housing prices but negatively correlates with inflation. The only notable difference between the model and the sample is the correlation between housing prices and residential investment. The model reports a high correlation (0.54) between these two variables, suggesting that demand shocks primarily drive the housing market; however, the sample has a correlation value close to zero. While Fehrle (2019) suggests that housing capital utilization and adjustment cost in the model can generate a positive correlation between housing prices and residential investment, as reported here, the sample correlation value near zero is indeed peculiar in Korea. It mainly arises from the negative correlation between two variables from 2010 to 2019: −0.31. Oh (2018) explains this negative correlation because residential investment during this period is driven by supply factors rather than demand factors. #### _5.1. Macroeconomic effects of regionally heterogeneous housing cycles_

#### _5.2. Region-specific stabilization policy_ 

Considering the historical use of those stabilization policy instruments, we examine the effect of the property tax, countercyclical LTV regulation, and housing supply policies on regionally heterogeneous housing cycles. The property tax rate should increase by approximately three times its steady-state value to accomplish this policy effect, which implies that the property tax rate rises from 1 percent to 4 percent. In addition, the figure suggests that a 100 percent reduction in the LTV limit, which leads _𝑚𝑡_ to reach an implementable lower bound of 0, fails to achieve a 5 percent reduction in housing prices. This approach can also pose challenges for the government in two ways. First, it is unclear whether any housing deregulation can achieve this productivity gain for housing producers. #### _5.3. Monetary policy: leaning against the wind_ 

Because rising housing prices and household debt can pose financial or inflation stability risks, central banks can tighten monetary policy during a housing boom in a leaning against the wind fashion. Here, we discuss the effect of monetary policy when it is used as a stabilization instrument against a regional housing cycle. Whereas the policy instruments in the previous subsection are designed to operate region-specifically, monetary policy is assumed to focus on countrylevel financial stability. We assume that monetary policy systemically responds to national housing price inflation, where the national housing price index is defined as the weighted average of housing prices in regions 1 and 2. We set the value of _𝑟𝜋𝑞_ , the response of monetary policy to housing price inflation, to one: 



Fig. 7 illustrates the effect of leaning against the wind monetary policy against housing demand shocks in region 1. The first finding is that although this degree of response to housing price inflation is not considered weak, monetary policy has a minimal influence on housing price dynamics. This result contrasts with the previous stabilization policy instruments targeted at regional housing markets. Two factors can explain this inefficacy of monetary policy on regional housing prices. First, monetary policy responds to national housing price inflation, not regional. As national housing price inflation is weaker than the regional one, the strength of monetary tightening is also weaker. Second, the effects of monetary policy on households are dispersed 

to housing markets in other regions and consumption. Fig. 7 suggests that monetary policy has significant implications for consumption and welfare in both regions, supporting the second channel. 

The leaning against the wind monetary policy has asymmetric effects on borrowers and savers. As expected, a higher interest rate during housing appreciations negatively impacts the consumption and housing of both regions’ borrowers. In contrast, the income effect from the higher interest rate enables both regions’ savers to increase consumption and housing. As a result, this monetary policy improves welfare for savers and reduces welfare for borrowers in all regions. 

Fig. 8 shows the effect of leaning against the wind monetary policy against housing supply shocks in region 1. Again, the effect of monetary policy on housing price dynamics is minimal. The monetary policy reduces the consumption and housing of borrowers in both regions and lowers their welfare. As the housing cycle generated by housing supply shocks is persistent, monetary policy tightening and the adverse welfare effects on borrowers are more persistent as well. Monetary policy also slightly reduces savers’ consumption during the early periods of the housing cycle; however, savers can increase their housing stock, and their welfare also rises. 

To summarize, using monetary policy to stabilize regionally heterogeneous housing booms is ineffective in lowering housing prices and has a spillover effect on other regions that do not experience housing booms. The effect is asymmetric between saving and borrowing households. Monetary tightening by a higher interest rate harms creditconstrained households, especially their consumption, housing, and welfare. In contrast, it benefits saving households in all regions.

#### **7. Conclusion** 

This study investigates the macroeconomic implications of regionally heterogeneous housing cycles arising from idiosyncratic shocks in regional housing markets. Their effect on welfare varies by the types of shocks. Increasing demand in the regional housing market can cause regional consumption and housing booms and welfare-improving for borrowers in the region. Rising housing prices in the region because of low housing supply is welfare-reducing for all agents in the region, as they suffer from low housing services. Regional housing shocks also affect agents in other regions, primarily through borrowers’ housing investment in the region that experiences the cycles. Therefore, regionally heterogeneous cycles can be a source of economic inequality across regions and levels of financial status. 

Regionally heterogeneous housing cycles challenge national policymakers as welfare implications differ across regions and the type of shocks that cause the cycle. Responding by nationwide policy measures might be inappropriate, as this study’s monetary policy is ineffective in stabilizing housing cycles. 

This study’s analysis enables the comparison of the region-specific housing market stabilization measures that the Korean government 

implemented. Among these tools, although the effect varies depending on the source of the housing boom, the housing supply policy provides the best option considering its effect on regional housing prices and welfare. This policy includes deregulations on housing construction or government expenditure to increase the public housing supply; however, caution is needed because the policy can cause other social problems, such as traffic and environmental issues. In contrast, the LTV policy imposes a substantial burden on borrowers in the region experiencing a housing boom.

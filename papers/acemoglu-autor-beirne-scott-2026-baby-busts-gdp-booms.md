# Baby busts and GDP booms: Demographic change and the macroeconomy

**Source:** CEPR VoxEU Column, 30 July 2026
**Authors:** Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne, Andrew Scott
**Based on:** CEPR Discussion Paper 21673
**URL:** https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/baby-busts-and-gdp-booms-demographic-change-and-macroeconomy

---

## Abstract

Globally declining birth rates have, over the last seven decades, slowed population growth and raised average ages. This column argues that despite widespread economic pessimism, historical cross-country and US commuting zone data point to no impact of ageing populations on aggregate GDP or earnings growth. Countries with lower birth rates have higher GDP growth per working-age adult, and US commuting zones with lower birth rates have faster wage growth. The growth-reducing potential of demographic slowdowns is countered by faster technological change. While future demographic changes will occur at greater speed and scale, the economic record suggests that current pessimism is premature.

## Key Findings

### 1. Global Birth Rate Decline
- 1950: global crude birth rate = 3.78 per 100 people
- 2025: declined to 1.71 (more than halved)
- Lowest today: China (0.63), Japan (0.60), South Korea (0.46)
- UN projects absolute global population decline in second half of 21st century (first since bubonic plague)

### 2. Cross-Country Evidence (1950–2020, countries >1M pop)
- Countries with lower birth rates → **higher GDP growth per working-age adult**
- No significant impact on aggregate GDP growth or aggregate earnings
- Magnitude: 1 p.p. lower birth rate → ~22% higher GDP per worker after 40 years, 29% after 60 years

### 3. US Commuting Zone Evidence (722 zones)
- Zones with lower birth rates → **faster average wage growth**
- Employment shifts toward R&D-intensive industries
- Increase in labour-saving patenting

### 4. Mechanism: Directed Technological Change
- Labour scarcity induces technological innovation
- Countries with largest fertility declines → largest shifts to high-tech exports/employment
- WWII evidence: military deaths (reduce young workers) → higher GDP per worker; civilian deaths → lower GDP per worker
- Parallels: French regions with higher WWI military mortality → increased labour-saving patenting (Bergeaud et al. 2025)

### 5. Channels Ruled Out
- Female labour force participation — no consistent support
- Quantity-quality tradeoff in child-rearing — no consistent support
- Structural transformation (agriculture to manufacturing) — no consistent support
- Results distinct from "demographic dividend" literature (long-lasting across decades)

## Implications for Housing Economics

### Demand Side
- **Lower birth rates → slower household formation** → reduced housing demand in long run
- But: **higher GDP per worker → higher incomes** → higher housing demand per household
- Net effect depends on relative magnitudes

### Supply Side
- Labour scarcity in construction → **induced innovation in construction technology** (prefab, modular, automation)
- Higher wages in construction → cost-push pressure on housing prices
- R&D-intensive industries clustering → local housing demand in innovation hubs

### Demographic Structure
- Ageing population → shift in housing preferences (downsizing, accessibility, proximity to healthcare)
- Older workers (45+) growing share of workforce (projected 60% in Korea/Japan/China by 2050)
- **Silver economy** housing: senior living, multigenerational, age-in-place modifications

### China Case Study
- Working-age population projected to fall from 1B peak to ~300M by 2100
- China leads in industrial robotics (50% global stock, doubled 2021–2024)
- Directed technological change channel very active
- Open question: will it be sufficient to stabilise GDP growth at scale of demographic challenge?

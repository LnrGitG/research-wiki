## **Abstract** 

Global housing demand is rising rapidly, yet the combined pressures of future population growth, climate-driven hazard intensification, and constrained fiscal capacity have not been assessed together for their impact on housing at a global scale. Housing is one of the largest components of household wealth and national capital stock. This study provides the first global, country-level picture of future housing construction demand and its associated costs and identifies where the needs for resilient construction are most urgent. The lack of high-resolution, globally consistent geospatial and demographic data has limited previous analyses. This study integrates Shared Socioeconomic Pathways–based population projections to 2100 with dynamic household size modeling, cohort survival analysis, and multi-hazard exposure mapping to estimate future housing construction demand and its associated costs under business-as-usual and resilient construction pathways. Global housing stock is projected to nearly double by 2100, reaching 4.1 billion to 4.5 billion units. Resilient construction pathways are 

found to carry lower long-term costs than business-as-usual in most regions, although not in high-growth, high-hazard regions such as South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. Construction demand in most regions is projected to transition from growth-driven to replacement-driven by mid-century, suggesting that the decisions made today about how and where it is built will shape countries’ exposure to natural hazards and their fiscal obligations for decades to come. The study further identifies 46 countries facing a triple burden: high future housing demand, severe natural hazard exposure, and limited fiscal capacity to finance resilient construction. The nature of this burden varies systematically by region, with Pacific Island countries facing acute fiscal constraints, Sub-Saharan Africa facing unprecedented volumetric demand, and South and Southeast Asian countries facing the highest hazard exposure. These findings provide a quantitative basis for prioritizing resilient housing investment, nationally and through international support, differentiated across regions and individual countries.

# **1. Introduction** 

Over the coming decades, the world will construct an enormous volume of new housing, concentrated in regions where natural hazard exposure is high and institutional capacity to regulate and enforce safe construction is limited. Housing is typically the largest asset households own and one of the largest components of the built environment, and its quality shapes health outcomes, poverty dynamics, and the scale of losses when disasters strike. The decisions embedded in that construction, such as where to build, at what quality, and to what hazard-resistance standard, will determine disaster risk and development outcomes for housing, people, and livelihoods for the remainder of this century. Current estimates place the global housing deficit at approximately 268 million units, affecting 1.26 billion people across 64 emerging economies (Behr et al., 2021), and national assessments consistently suggest this figure understates the true scope. Across the globe, housing deficits are large, growing, and structurally embedded in patterns of urbanization, income inequality, and limited institutional capacity. 

The housing deficit has two dimensions, and both are relevant to disaster risk. The quantitative deficit refers to the shortage of available dwellings relative to the number of households requiring housing. Nigeria estimates a shortage of 17 million to 30 million units (World Bank, 2016), India projects a shortage of 30 million units by 2030 (Knight Frank & NAREDCO, 2025), and Indonesia requires approximately 780,000 new units annually through 2045 (World Bank, 2020). The qualitative deficit refers to households living in substandard or structurally inadequate housing, such as dwellings with insecure foundations, incomplete walls or roofing, or severe overcrowding, and this dimension is often larger than the quantitative one. In Colombia, 73 percent of the total housing deficit is qualitative in nature (World Bank, 2021); in El Salvador, 72 percent of households experience some form of housing deprivation (World Bank, 2024). These figures are not only welfare indicators but also a measure of structural vulnerability, as the conditions that define the qualitative deficit are the same conditions that determine how housing performs when hazards occur. 

Housing demand is not driven by population growth alone, and this distinction matters for understanding where and how fast the deficit will expand. Global population is projected to peak at approximately 10.3 billion people in the mid-2080s (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2024), but a more immediate pressure comes from the structural decline in household size (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2019). The global average household size has declined by approximately 0.5 person per decade since 1970 (Esteve et al., 2024). As incomes rise and family structures evolve, more households form from the same population base, amplifying demand beyond what population projections alone would imply. In developing economies, this additional 

2 

demand is unlikely to be absorbed by formal housing markets, which remain inaccessible to low-income households due to high costs, restrictive financing, and limited serviced land supply. More than 1 billion people already live in informal settlements (UN-Habitat, 2025), and the conditions that produced those settlements, including constrained land markets, limited institutional capacity, and low household incomes, remain largely unchanged. 

The tendency for informal and low-quality housing to concentrate in hazard-prone areas is not incidental. It reflects the logic of land markets that systematically exclude low-income households from safe, well-serviced locations, directing them instead toward floodplains, steep slopes, coastal zones, and seismically active areas that formal markets have rejected precisely because of their risk profiles. Empirical evidence confirms this pattern across regions: informal settlements and substandard housing are disproportionately located in areas of high hazard exposure (Dodman et al., 2022; Rentschler et al., 2022). As long as the underlying conditions driving informal settlement persist, new housing demand will continue to be absorbed in these same locations. This pattern follows the standard logic of disaster risk: informal construction in hazard-prone areas increases exposure, while poverty and limited access to insurance or savings constrain coping capacity, compounding vulnerability and producing higher overall risk. 

The cost of this overlap is substantial and growing. According to the 2025 Global Assessment Report (GAR): Resilience Pays: Financing and Investing, annual disaster costs now exceed $2.3 trillion when cascading and ecosystem losses are included (United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction, 2025). A significant portion of these costs is attributable to housing losses. Building quality is a central factor: a large share of the existing stock was built without adherence to building codes, without technical oversight, and with materials that perform poorly under hazard stress (World Bank et al., 2025). Households in inadequate housing are more exposed to hazard events, suffer greater losses when events occur, have fewer resources for recovery, and are more likely to rebuild in the same hazardous locations (Hallegatte et al., 2016). Without a shift in the conditions governing where and how housing is built, this cycle will continue to generate disaster damage and losses at scale. 

The body of research on housing and disaster risk has established a robust historical baseline. Studies document that 82 percent of new high-risk settlements between 1985 and 2015 occurred in low- and middle-income countries (Rentschler et al., 2023), and that the population exposed to floods alone increased by 20 to 24 percent between 2000 and 2018 (Tellman et al., 2021). What these studies do not address is the forward-looking dimension: where housing will be built, at what quality, and under what hazard conditions through the end of this century, and across the full range of relevant hazards including 

3 

earthquakes, tropical storms, and extreme heat. This gap has been partly a data limitation. Until recently, spatially explicit population projections at fine resolution beyond 2030 were unavailable. The release of the GHSL World Urbanisation Prospects (GHS-WUP) and FuturePop datasets now provides 1 km grid projections through 2100 under multiple SSP scenarios, making this analysis tractable. These datasets provide population and demographic projections, not housing projections directly; converting population into housing units requires the household size and cohort survival modelling described below. Building on these data, this study addresses three gaps in prior work in order to understand how future housing demand, hazard exposure, and construction quality will jointly evolve at global scale: the lack of spatial disaggregation in housing projections; the absence of simultaneous integration of demographic, hazard, and building quality dynamics at global scale; and the poorly quantified long-term cost implications of alternative construction quality pathways. It further introduces a classification of countries facing a triple burden of acute housing demand, disproportionate hazard exposure, and limited fiscal capacity to finance resilient construction. These three factors are selected because together they determine whether a country can convert housing need into resilient housing supply: demand sets the scale of construction required, hazard exposure determines the stakes of building it poorly, and fiscal capacity determines whether resilient construction is financially attainable at all. 

This study specifically answers the following five research questions: 

1. Where and when will new housing be built globally through 2100? 

2. What proportion of future housing will be located in natural hazard-prone areas, across earthquakes, floods, tropical storms, and extreme heat? 

3. How do alternative building quality pathways affect long-term housing resilience and replacement needs? 

4. What are the cost implications of business-as-usual versus resilient construction? 

5. Which countries face a convergence of high housing demand, severe hazard exposure, and limited fiscal capacity, and what does this imply for international policy support? 

Together, these questions provide a global, forward-looking picture of where housing risk will concentrate and where resilient construction support is most urgently needed. 

# **2. Data** 

The analysis integrated multiple globally comprehensive datasets spanning demographic projections, housing characteristics, and natural hazard indicators. All spatial data were harmonized to a common Mollweide equal-area projection (ESRI:54009) at 1km resolution to ensure consistent spatial calculations across diverse data sources. 

4 

### **Demographic Data** 

**Population Projection** : The study utilized two complementary sources for spatially explicit population projections. The Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) from the European Commission's Joint Research Center provides population grids for multiple time horizons based on historical settlement patterns and demographic models (Schiavina et al., 2026). WorldPop datasets offer alternative population projections with different Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios (Bondarenko et al., 2025). Both datasets provide 1 km gridded population estimates in 5-year intervals from 2025 to 2100. 

**Household Size Data** : The study used three complementary data sources to obtain the most disaggregated household size information available worldwide. The primary data came from the CORESIDENCE Database, Version 2. This database provides subnationallevel household size estimates for 136 countries including 1,482 administrative units (Galeano et al., 2024). The second source was the national-level average household size estimates from the same database, which added 21 more countries to the list. The third source was the country-level household size estimates derived from United Nations demographic databases (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2022), which added 33 countries. For countries without household size information, the regional average, as defined by the World Bank region classification, was used. 

**Demographic Projections** The Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) Scenario Database from the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis (IIASA) provides tabular demographic projections, including population breakdowns by age group, for each country under five SSP scenarios through the year 2100 (Riahi et al., 2017). 

### **Housing Data** 

**Housing Quality and Cost:** The Global Earthquake Model (GEM) Foundation's Global Exposure Model provides subnational building inventory data that characterizes the quality of residential buildings through a standardized taxonomy (Yepes-Estrada et al., 2026; Yepes-Estrada et al., 2023). This dataset is structured as a table at the subnational level, which was spatially joined to subnational administrative boundary polygons to enable geographic analysis. The World Bank's Unbreakable study further classifies housing stock into three quality tiers (fragile, median, and robust) based on structural characteristics and construction materials (Middelanis et al., 2025). 

### **Boundary and Economic Data** 

**Administrative Boundaries:** The country and first-level administrative boundaries were derived from the World Bank's Global Administrative Database. This database provides standardized vector geometries for 244 countries and territories (World Bank, 2025). These 

5 

boundaries were processed in the ESRI:54009 projection and rasterized to create a master grid template. This template enables consistent aggregation from 1-km pixel-level calculations to administrative units. 

**GDP and Urbanization Projection:** The SSP Scenario Database from the IIASA provides GDP and urbanization projections for each of the five SSP scenarios up to the year 2100 (Chen et al., 2022). 

### **Natural Hazard Data** 

This study constructed spatially explicit binary hazard masks for four natural hazards (flood, earthquake, tropical cyclone wind, and extreme heat) at 1 km² resolution aligned to the master population grid. Each hazard was assessed at a primary threshold representing meaningful structural or health risk to residential buildings, with a stricter secondary threshold used as a sensitivity check to test whether results are robust to a more conservative definition of hazard exposure. 

**Flood Hazard** : Flood exposure was characterized using the World Resources Institute Aqueduct Flood Hazard Maps version 2 (World Resources Institute, 2020), accessed through Google Earth Engine. The dataset provides gridded projections of riverine and coastal inundation depth at approximately 1 km resolution across multiple return periods and climate scenarios. This study extracted 100-year return period flood depth projections for 2030, 2050, and 2080 under two climate forcing scenarios: RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5. For riverine flooding, projections represent the ensemble mean across four general circulation models: NorESM1-M, GFDL-ESM2M, HadGEM2-ES, and IPSL-CM5A-LR. For coastal flooding, projections adopt the median sea level rise trajectory with land subsidence incorporated. The two components were merged at the pixel level by taking the maximum inundation depth, in order to produce a single composite flood exposure measure suitable for a global, multi-hazard comparison alongside earthquake, storm, and heat hazards. Grid cells with projected inundation depth exceeding 0.15 meters were classified as floodprone. A secondary threshold of 0.5 meters was applied as a sensitivity check. 

For the housing projection scenarios, SSP1 and SSP2 were paired with RCP 4.5, while SSP3, SSP4, and SSP5 were paired with RCP 8.5. Flood hazard layers are matched to housing projection years using the nearest available time slice: the 2030 layer is applied to projection years 2025 and 2030, the 2050 layer to 2040 and 2050, and the 2080 layer to 2060 through 2100. This analysis does not separately capture pluvial flooding, which can affect urban areas independently of river or coastal proximity. As a result, flood exposure in this study, particularly in dense urban areas, may be understated. Incorporating pluvial flood hazard is an area for future refinement as higher-resolution global datasets become available. 

6 

**Seismic Hazard** : Earthquake exposure was characterized using GEM's Global Seismic Hazard Map, which provides peak ground acceleration (PGA) with a 10 percent probability of being exceeded in 50 years return period at approximately 10 km resolution (Johnson et al., 2023). Grid cells where PGA exceeds 0.2g were classified as earthquake hazard zones, where structural damage becomes probable for non-engineered construction. A secondary threshold of 0.4g, associated with severe structural damage, was used as a sensitivity check. 

**Storm Hazard** : Wind hazard from tropical cyclones was assessed using the STORM tropical cyclone wind speed return period datasets version 4 (Nadia Bloemendaal et al., 2023; Nadia; Bloemendaal et al., 2023). The dataset provides 100-year return period maximum sustained 10-minute wind speeds at approximately 10 km resolution. Two temporal layers were used. For projection years 2025 and 2030, the historical baseline layer is applied. For 2040 onwards, a future wind speed layer representing conditions at approximately 2050 was constructed by computing the pixel-wise ensemble mean across four models: CMCC-CM2-VHR4, CNRM-CM6-1-HR, EC-Earth3P-HR, and HadGEM3-GC31HM. Grid cells with projected wind speeds exceeding 33 m/s (corresponding to Category 1 hurricane equivalent sustained winds) were classified as exposed to storm hazard. A secondary threshold of 42 m/s, broadly corresponding to Category 2 hurricane equivalent sustained winds, was applied as a sensitivity check. 

**Extreme Heat Hazard** : Heat hazard was assessed using the NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 climate projection (approximately at 25km resolution), accessed through Google Earth Engine. Daily near-surface air temperature and relative humidity outputs from five models (GFDLESM4, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, and UKESM1-0-LL) were processed for two climate scenarios: SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. The heat metric used was the indoor wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) of the hottest calendar month within a decadal window centered on each projection year. Indoor WBGT was estimated using the formula: 

𝑊𝐵𝐺𝑇𝑖𝑛𝑑𝑜𝑜𝑟 = 0.7 × 𝑇𝑤 + 0.3 × 𝑇𝑎 

where 𝑇𝑤 is the psychrometric wet-bulb temperature derived from air temperature and relative humidity following (Stull, 2011), and 𝑇𝑎 is dry-bulb air temperature. 

Monthly mean Indoor WBGT was computed for each model across each decadal window. For example, data from 2035 to 2044 were used for the 2040 projection year. For 2100, data from 2095 to 2100 were used given the data unavailability beyond 2100. Then, the hottestmonth mean WBGT was averaged across the five models to produce a single estimate for each SSP scenario and projection year. This metric captures chronic heat stress in indoor residential environments during the peak seasonal period, which is directly relevant to 

7 

habitability and labor productivity in housing without mechanical cooling. Grid cells where the hottest-month indoor WBGT exceeds 32°C were classified as extreme heat hazard zones. A secondary threshold of 35°C was applied as a sensitivity check. SSP1 and SSP2 use the SSP2-4.5 heat projections, and SSP3, SSP4, and SSP5 use the SSP5-8.5 projections.

As reported in Annex 2, the OLS coefficients closely track the betweencountry (cross-sectional) effects. A random slopes multilevel model further supports the assumption of a globally homogeneous slope. ### **Stage 3: Dynamic Cohort Survival and Housing Quality Evolution** 

The third stage used a recursive dynamic cohort survival model to track the evolution of housing stock quality and construction volumes from 2025 through 2100 across three structural quality tiers: Fragile, Median, and Robust. Initial quality distributions were derived from the Global Exposure Model. The nighttime population attribute in GEM served as a proxy for occupied dwellings, and the proportional shares of fragile, median, and robust stock were computed for each subnational region and disaggregated to the 1 km² grid. The model advanced in five-year steps for year 2025-2030 and ten-year steps for the remaining projection period, where 𝑛 denotes the length of each simulation interval in years.

The quality composition of new construction was determined by the policy scenario. Under the Business-as-Usual (BAU) scenario, new construction replicated the typology mix of the preceding stock, embedding structural inertia: regions with predominantly fragile housing continue to build fragile housing absent policy intervention. Under the Resilient Policy scenario, the lowest-quality tier was progressively phased out of new construction through a transition matrix applied to each new cohort:

This ensures that structural upgrading is gradual, with the pace governed endogenously by existing stock composition and replacement cycles. Alternative scenarios, consisting of higher governance and lower governance implementation capacities (75 percent upgrade and 25 percent upgrade, respectively), were tested as sensitivity analysis. 

### **Stage 4: Hazard Exposure Assessment** 

The fourth stage overlaid dynamic housing stock projections with natural hazard layers to quantify physical exposure of housing units to climate-related and geophysical risks. All 

11 

hazard layers were resampled to the 1km² grid using nearest-neighbor interpolation to ensure pixel-level alignment. 

Exposure was assessed across two dimensions. Stock exposure measured the total number of housing units located within hazard zones during each simulation period, disaggregated by structural quality tier, characterizing the accumulated physical risk in the built environment at a given point in time. Construction flow exposure isolated newly constructed units within hazard zones at each simulation step, revealing where development is actively expanding the risk frontier.  This includes both densification within existing built-up areas, such as replacement of lower-density structures with higherdensity ones, and greenfield development in areas not previously urbanized. Both pathways represent a meaningful increase in exposure, particularly when expansion occurs in flood-prone or otherwise hazard-affected locations. 

To distinguish these two pathways, a threshold of 300 inhabitants per 1 km² grid cell was applied following established global urban-rural classifications (Dijkstra et al., 2021).  Cells below this threshold were classified as candidates for greenfield development when net housing expansion was projected within them, with direct implications for land policy, cadastral planning, and land entitlement processes. Cells above the threshold were treated as existing built-up areas where densification contributes to construction flow exposure. 

### **Stage 5: Affordability of Resilient Measures** 

The fifth stage translated physical construction projections into economic cost estimates and evaluated the fiscal feasibility of transitioning from BAU to resilient track. Unit replacement costs were derived from the GEM dataset at country level and adjusted for future periods using projected GDP per capita (PPP). Cumulative construction volumes by quality tier from Stage 3 were annualized by dividing period totals by the number of years in each simulation interval, and unit costs were then applied. Annualized costs were benchmarked against projected national GDP under each SSP scenario, expressed in PPP terms to facilitate cross-country comparison. 

### **Stage 6: Triple Burden Classification** 

The sixth stage constructed a composite index to identify countries where three structural disadvantages converge simultaneously. Three indicators were derived from scenario model outputs: total projected housing unit requirements between 2025 and 2100 as a share of 2025 stock (demand pressure); the share of cumulative new construction located in high-risk zones across four hazard types (earthquake, flood, storm, and heat), capturing hazard exposure; and the peak annual incremental cost of resilient construction as a share 

12 

of GDP, capturing affordability constraints. Benchmarking incremental construction costs against GDP follows standard practice in the fiscal space literature, where a government's capacity to absorb a given spending obligation is assessed relative to the overall size of its economy (Kose et al., 2022). 

Each of the three indicators was normalized using min-max scaling to place them on a common zero-to-one scale, removing differences in absolute magnitude across indicators and countries. For the hazard exposure indicator specifically, which aggregates across four distinct hazard types (earthquake, flood, storm, and heat), an additional normalization step was required: each hazard's raw exposure rate was first scaled to zero-to-one using minmax normalization, and the resulting scores were then converted to percentile ranks to equalize the distributional influence of each hazard type before they were averaged into a single hazard exposure score. For each indicator, the worst-case value across all six demographic scenarios (GHSL, SSP1-SSP5) was retained per country. The three worst-case scores were then normalized and averaged into an unweighted composite index across all countries. Countries in the top quartile were considered triple burden countries. 

### **Sensitivity Analysis** 

The model was evaluated under three sensitivity dimensions alongside the baseline: building lifespan assumptions, governance implementation capacity, and natural hazard classification thresholds. All sensitivity runs used SSP3. 

Building lifespan assumptions are among the most consequential parameters in the model, as replacement demand constitutes the majority of total global construction need throughout the projection horizon. Three parameter sets were evaluated: a short lifespan variant (Fragile = 10 years, Medium = 30 years, Robust = 50 years), the baseline (Fragile = 20 years, Medium = 40 years, Robust = 60 years), and a long lifespan variant (Fragile = 25 years, Medium = 50 years, Robust = 75 years). 

Governance implementation capacity determines the rate at which newly constructed housing under the Resilient pathway meets higher quality standards. The baseline assumed moderate implementation (50 percent upgrades among new construction). Two alternative scenarios were evaluated: low implementation (25 percent upgrades, reflecting weak regulatory enforcement) and high implementation (75 percent upgrades, reflecting strong institutional capacity and enforcement). 

Natural hazard thresholds define the exposure level at which a housing unit was classified as at risk. The baseline thresholds reflected moderate return-period events. A stricter second-threshold variant was evaluated using more severe exposure levels: heat stress at 35 degrees Celsius indoor wet-bulb global temperature equivalent, flood depth at 0.5 

13 

meters, wind speed at 42 meters per second, and peak ground acceleration at 0.4g for seismic hazard. 

### **Model Validation** 

To assess the predictive accuracy of the household size projection model, the study applied a temporal hold-out validation approach that mirrors the full projection pipeline. This approach tests whether the model, when trained only on older data, can accurately predict outcomes it has not yet seen. The historical dataset is split at a cutoff year: observations before the cutoff are used to re-fit the model and compute offsets, while observations from the cutoff onward are set aside and used only to check the model's predictions against actual outcomes, rather than being used in the fitting process itself. The validation covers both subnational and national units following the same hierarchy used in the projection: subnational units are validated where subnational data exist on both sides of the cutoff, and national-level units cover the remaining countries. The study uses 2005 as the primary cutoff, providing a hold-out window of approximately 17 years, and 2008 as a secondary cutoff to test robustness.

# **5. Discussion** 

This study projected that the global housing stock will nearly double by the year 2100, reaching between 4.1 billion and 4.5 billion units, depending on the scenario. Notably, population size alone cannot predict aggregate housing requirements across scenarios. Although the population under SSP3 is 4.9 billion larger than under SSP5 by 2100, the required housing stock is similar in both scenarios. This reflects how rising incomes under SSP5 lead to smaller households, effectively offsetting the lower population count. The implication for policy makers is that strategies for housing investment calibrated to 

31 

population projections alone risk systematic error, particularly in economies with high income growth. 

The construction quality decisions made in the coming decades will determine the burden of replacing housing for the rest of the century. Throughout this study, the Resilient pathway refers to a scenario in which new construction progressively shifts away from fragile building typologies toward median- and robust-quality standards. Fragile buildings have shorter service lives and require earlier replacement, which compounds fiscal pressure when new construction demand remains high. The resilient construction pathway reduces annual replacement requirements by 34 to 40 percent compared to business as usual by the 2090s. In most regions, construction expenditure under the resilient pathway converges with and then falls below the BAU trajectory between 2060 and 2070. This expenditure crossover, however, should be read alongside the scope of the analysis: the model captures construction expenditure only and does not quantify avoided disaster losses, reduced maintenance burdens, or the welfare costs of continued fragile housing. The durability effect alone drives the crossover observed in low-to-moderate growth regions. In high-growth, high-hazard regions such as South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, where sustained construction volumes prevent the crossover from materializing within the century, the full economic return to resilient investment is more likely to be realized through avoided losses than through lower construction volumes. Assessing that return would require a separate benefit estimation and remains an important direction for future work. 

The heterogeneity among the 46 triple burden countries has direct implications for national policy as well as international support. In countries where fiscal incapacity is the main constraint, such as Pacific small island states, national governments have comparatively limited room to act alone, and concessional financing and dedicated climate finance mechanisms are the priority, since reducing the upfront cost premium of resilient construction is what determines whether the long-term savings from lower replacement costs are ever realized. In regions where demand pressure dominates, such as much of Sub-Saharan Africa, the most urgent need is to establish and enforce building standards before the majority of construction occurs, since regulatory action taken now applies to construction that has not yet occurred. In regions where hazard exposure is the primary driver, such as South and Southeast Asia, spatial planning and land use policy are as important as construction quality, because a well-built house in a highly exposed location still carries substantial residual risk. It is worth clarifying that this study measures hazard exposure, meaning the physical presence of housing stock in zones of hazard occurrence, rather than disaster risk in the fuller sense, which also depends on the vulnerability of specific structures and populations and the value of what stands to be lost. 

32 

Several limitations should be noted. First, hazard projections were not fully aligned with population projection scenarios across all reference years, requiring temporal interpolation and scenario-matching assumptions that introduce uncertainty. Flood hazard mapping at 1 km resolution is also likely insufficient to capture local exposure differences in topographically complex or densely urbanized areas. Second, the analysis captures housing requirements driven by demographics rather than effective demand based on purchasing power, and the projections therefore represent an upper-bound estimate of need rather than a forecast of actual construction. The affordability assessment operates at the country level and does not reflect the distribution of constraints across individual households. Third, the building lifespan assumptions are held uniform across all contexts; in practice, effective service lives vary with the speed and form of urbanization and differ between urban and rural settings. 

Beyond these data and measurement constraints, several modeling choices also bear on how the results should be interpreted. The resilient scenario assumes full replacement of fragile stock rather than partial retrofitting or incremental rehabilitation, which is the more common pathway in informal settlements; the cost and effectiveness profiles of progressive improvement programs are not captured in the current framework. The model also does not account for disaster shocks or climate-driven migration, both of which can alter the spatial distribution and timing of housing demand in ways that smooth depreciation functions cannot represent. The three burden dimensions are, in addition, treated as independent in the composite index, but climate change is likely to drive both hazard exposure and, through recurrent disaster losses, the erosion of fiscal capacity in exposed countries, an interaction this study does not capture and one that implies the fiscal burden facing high hazard-exposure countries may be understated rather than overstated. Finally, the equal weighting of the triple burden index components is a normative choice; in most policy applications, the individual burden scores are more directly actionable than the composite, and alternative weighting schemes could alter country classifications at the margin. 

# **6. Conclusion** 

This study is the first to provide a spatially explicit, multi-hazard, multi-scenario assessment that links housing demand, disaster risk exposure, and fiscal capacity across 181 countries through the year 2100. The results show that long-term resilient construction expenditure falls below business as usual in most regions, driven by the durability effect of higher-quality stock reducing replacement volumes over time. The transition from population growth–driven to replacement-driven construction will redefine the housing challenge in most regions by mid-century. Finally, the triple burden classification offers 

33 

international development partners a quantitative, regionally differentiated basis for prioritizing support, with Pacific small island states facing acute fiscal constraints, SubSaharan Africa facing an unprecedented volumetric demand challenge, and South and Southeast Asian countries facing the highest hazard exposure as the primary risk driver. 

The global framework developed here is designed as a foundation for more granular applications. Translating the methodology to the country and city levels using local building stock inventories, local hazard assessments, and specific demographic and fiscal parameters would substantially improve the framework's accuracy. This would allow the results to be used directly for housing sector strategies, urban planning decisions, and disaster risk management policies in an integrated way. At that scale, the analysis can inform how much housing is needed, where it should be located, what standards are appropriate, what fiscal pathways are realistic, and how land use and risk reduction investments should be sequenced. The author considers this localization to be the most important direction for future work. Ultimately, most of the housing that will stand in 2100 has not yet been built, and the decisions on standards and location made over the next decades will shape disaster risk, fiscal exposure, and the quality of life for billions of people for the remainder of the century.

### **Annex 2: Household Size Projection Specification Robustness Tests** 

This annex presents four complementary tests to assess whether pooled OLS is an appropriate specification for long-run structural projection of average household size (AHS). The tests follow a logical sequence: a Hausman-style comparison of OLS and country fixed effects (Section A2.1); a Mundlak-Chamberlain correlated random effects test to decompose within-country and between-country effects (Section A2.2); a four-way coefficient comparison across OLS, fixed effects, between effects, and within-OLS specifications (Section A2.3); and a random slopes multilevel model to assess the degree of cross-country slope heterogeneity (Section A2.4). 

The dataset covers 172 countries from 1990 to 2021 (N = 984). Countries with only a single observation were excluded from fixed effects estimation, as identification requires withincountry temporal variation, reducing the FE sample to 966 observations. 

### A2.1 Hausman-Style Test: OLS versus Country Fixed Effects 

A Hausman-style test was conducted to assess whether systematic differences exist between OLS and country fixed effects (FE) coefficient estimates. The Hausman test statistic is H = 64.88 (df = 4, p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis that OLS and FE estimates are equivalent. This indicates that country-level unobserved heterogeneity is correlated with the regressors. This is as expected in a cross-country panel spanning diverse economic and demographic contexts. This result alone does not determine which specification is more appropriate for projection. FE identifies coefficients from withincountry temporal variation, while OLS captures the cross-country structural gradient. For long-run projection to 2100, the cross-country gradient is the more relevant signal. Sections A2.2 and A2.3 examine this point in more detail and provide the empirical basis for retaining OLS as the main projection specification. 

### A2.2 Between-Effects and Within-Effects Decomposition 

To assess whether OLS coefficients are driven by cross-country differences or withincountry temporal change, a Mundlak-Chamberlain correlated random effects test was conducted by adding country-level means of each regressor to a random effects model. A likelihood ratio test confirms that these country means are jointly significant (LR = 33.69, df = 4, p < 0.001), with age structure variables (child rate and elderly rate) as the main source of divergence between OLS and FE estimates. Table A2.5 reports coefficients from four specifications side by side. 

The OLS coefficients are consistently closer to the between-effects estimates than to the FE estimates, and the between-effects R² (0.796) is the highest across all specifications. This confirms that OLS is predominantly driven by cross-country structural differences,

# **Housing market responses to the mortgage interest deduction** 

Sven Damen & Geert Goeyvaerts 

August, 2023 

##### **Abstract** 

This paper examines the impact of tax subsidies for homeowners on the housing market. In 2015, the Belgian regions became responsible for the tax subsidy after having been under federal control. The average present value of the subsidies decreased significantly more in Flanders than in Wallonia. We exploit this variation in a difference-in-differences design to estimate the effect of its repeal on equilibrium prices and quantities. There are two important results. Changes in the subsidies largely capitalize in the price of housing and land, while their quantities remain unaffected. These results indicate that the subsidies are largely captured by current property owners and can be gradually reduced without disrupting the market. 

**Keywords** : Mortgage interest deduction, Housing market, Economic incidence. 

Damen: Department of Accountancy and Finance, University of Antwerp. (E-mail: sven.damen@uantwerp.be) Goeyvaerts: Department of Economics, KU Leuven. (E-mail: geert.goeyvaerts@kuleuven.be)

## **1 Introduction** 

Policy-makers often seek to support the dream of many households to become homeowners. One of the most commonly used instruments to achieve this objective is the mortgage interest deduction (MID), which enables tax payers to deduct their mortgage interest payments from their personal income. However, a growing number of studies show that these tax subsidies have been largely ineffective. As a result, governments started to question the usefulness of the MID and looked at possible reforms.<sup>1</sup> 

We investigate the repeal of the mortgage payment deduction (MPD) in Belgium. The MPD allowed tax payers to deduct both the interest and principal payment up to a maximum amount. In 2014, the responsibility for the MPD was transferred to the Belgian regions, resulting in a series of reforms that reduced the average present value of the future tax subsidies substantially more in Flanders than in Wallonia.<sup>2</sup> We exploit this variation in a difference-in-differences design to obtain credible estimates of the effect of housing tax subsidies on the equilibrium price and quantity of housing and land. 

There are two main findings, both with important policy implications. First, the reduction in the discounted future tax subsidies for homeowners largely capitalizes in the price of housing and land. The estimates from our preferred specifications indicate that one euro decrease in the present value lowers the price of single-family houses by one euro and the price of plots of undeveloped land by eighty cents. This nearly full capitalization implies that property owners received the largest share of the subsidies through higher property prices.

## **3 Institutional background** 

Belgium tried to stimulate homeownership using a system similar to the mortgage interest deduction. The Belgian version of the MID was introduced in 2005.<sup>9</sup> It allowed tax payers to deduct annual mortgage payments from their taxable income.<sup>10</sup> We will therefore refer to this tax subsidy as the mortgage payment deduction (MPD). 

The size of the MPD depended on the size of the mortgage payment tax payers deducted and the tax rate at which it was deducted. The size of the mortgage payment that could be deducted was capped. In 2005, tax payers could deduct up to 1,870 Euro. They were allowed to deduct an additional 620 Euro during the first ten years of the mortgage, bringing the total deductible amount to 2,490 Euro.<sup>11</sup> These caps were adjusted to inflation every year. Table 1 reports the caps from 2005 to 2014. Aggregate data on the use of the MPD in Belgium in combination with survey data from the European Household Finance and Consumption Survey (HFCS) suggest that these caps were binding for most home buyers.<sup>12</sup> 

The mortgage payment was deducted from the taxable personal income, which mainly consists of labour income for employees or earnings in case the tax payer is self-employed.<sup>13</sup> There were five tax brackets in Belgium over the period from 2005 to 2014. Income in these brackets was taxed at the following rates: i) 25%, ii) 30%, iii) 40%, iv) 45% and v) 50%.<sup>14</sup> The use of the MPD was concentrated in the upper three tax brackets. The population of tax payers was distributed across the five tax brackets as follows: i) 23,4%, ii) 12,1%, iii) 26,5%, iv) 26,9% and v) 11,2%.<sup>15</sup> The population of tax payers that deduct a mortgage payment were distributed as follows: i) 8,9%, ii) 6,6%, iii) 20,5%, iv) 46,3%

### **4.1 Sales data** 

The information in the sales data comes from two sources: i) the notarial deed and ii) the land register. The deed provides information on the sale itself, like the date and price, and it provides some information on the property, like the type of property and the size of the plot. ### **4.2 Permit data** 

In the theoretical model, the equilibrium number of newly built houses is one-to-one related to the equilibrium number of sales of undeveloped plots. If this assumption holds in practice, the effect on sales of undeveloped plots will be the same as the effect on the number of newly built houses. In reality, however, developers do not immediately develop all the land they buy and they can develop land they already own for several years. When the demand for houses decreases due to a reduction in housing subsidies, the number of newly built houses might decrease more than the number of sales of undeveloped plots or even the other way around. ## **7 Conclusion** 

In this we studied the incidence of tax subsidies for homeowners. Reforms of paper, the Belgian mortgage payment deduction (MPD) created variation across regions and over time which we exploited to credibly identify the effect on the prices and quantities of housing and land. We reported two major findings. First, the average present value of future tax subsidies largely capitalizes into house and land prices. According to our preferred specifications, the capitalization rate is 100% for single family houses and 80% for land. In both cases, we cannot reject the null hypothesis of full capitalization. Second, the much larger reduction in the average value of the subsidy in Flanders did not decrease sales of existing houses, sales of plots of undeveloped land nor construction permits for single-family houses relative to Wallonia. 

The results in this paper show that the tax subsidies largely accrued to current property owners instead of future home buyers. However, the economic incidence of subsidies depends on the relative size of the price-elasticities of demand and supply, which can different dimensions. The literature the incidence of vary along studying non-recurrent tax subsidies for homeowners provides an interesting example. Berger et al. (2020) finds that the First-Time Homebuyer Credit (FTHC) increased sales in the U.S. by almost 10% with only a small increase in prices. In contrast, Krolage (2023) shows that a subsidy of similar size completely capitalized in listing prices in Bavaria. Importantly, the FTHC was introduced during the aftermath of the Great Recession. This was a time when housing demand was low as vacancies were on the rise and inventory was at historic levels. 

Due to data limitations, we were not able to examine heterogeneity along a couple of interesting dimensions. Future studies might be able to take into account. First, the relative size of the price-elasticities can vary over space. Hilber and Turner (2014) show that the mortgage subsidy rate is negatively correlated with homeownership and positively correlated with house prices in tightly regulated metropolitan areas, whereas it is 

32 

positively correlated with homeownership and not significantly correlated with prices in lightly regulated areas.<sup>54</sup> These results suggest that future home buyers benefit more from housing subsidies in areas where housing supply is more responsive. However, Baum-Snow and Han (2022) show that the price-elasticity of supply can vary significantly even within metropolitan areas. Future research could exploit increasingly granular data to study how the incidence varies with local supply conditions. 

Second, the relative size of the elasticities might vary over the income distribution. Davis (2019) shows that the effect of a one percentage point rise in the mortgage interest rate subsidy increases monotonically in house quality. Assuming house quality is strongly correlated with household income, these results suggest that buyers in the lower part of the income distribution benefit more from housing subsidies than buyers in the end of the distribution. Future research be able to link house sale upper may records to information on the socio-economic characteristics of the buyers to investigate heterogeneity in this dimension in more detail. 

Third, the relative size of the price-elasticities might vary over time. This paper finds that changes in the discounted value of future tax subsidies largely capitalize in prices over a period up to three years after the reforms. This period of time can be considered as the short-run. If the price-elasticity of supply of housing increases relative to the price-elasticity of demand over time, the rate at which the subsidies capitalize into house prices might decrease while the equilibrium quantity of housing increases. While challenging, future studies could try to examine the incidence over different periods in time.

### **A.3 Variation across tax brackets** 

The impact of the regional reforms varies across tax brackets. Figure A.11 plots the present value of the future annual tax subsidies per tax bracket (PV _r_ , _b_ , _t_ ) for Flanders (plot a) and Wallonia (plot b) together with the weighted average present value in each region (PV _r_ , _t_ ). In Flanders, the present value of the tax subsidies decreased for all tax payers except for those in the lowest tax bracket. In Wallonia, the present value decreased for tax in the three brackets while it increased for tax in the lowest payers upper payers two brackets. 

Figure A.12 plots the difference in the present value between Flanders and Wallonia per tax bracket. The difference in the present value between Flanders and Wallonia is quite similar for the four lower tax brackets. The present value of the future subsidies decreases more in Flanders than in Wallonia. The same holds for tax payers in the upper tax bracket in 2015. However, the present value for these tax payers decreases more in Wallonia than in Flanders after the introduction of the Chèque Habitat in 2016. 

In the empirical analysis, we exploit variation across the two regions and over time. The plots in Figure A.12 may raise the question whether we can also exploit variation across tax brackets. Gruber et al. (2021), for example, exploit variation across tax brackets to the effect of the interest deduction on identify mortgage homeownership, housing demand and indebtedness in Denmark. Contrary to Gruber and coauthors, however, we do not have any information on the buyers of the houses and plots of land. 

An alternative would be to variation across strategy exploit Belgian municipalities. There might be variation in the average present value of future tax subsidies due to variation in the share of tax payers per tax bracket. However, there are a couple of important problems to exploit variation at the municipal-level. First, there are too few sales to properly estimate the average change in quality-adjusted prices for each municipality. Second, this strategy is very vulnerable to spillover effects due to migration. The propensity to migrate is much larger at the municipal level than at the regional level.

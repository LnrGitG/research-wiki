Abstract
How can real-time information about aggregate economic activity be extracted from
firm-level accounting data?
This paper proposes a micro-to-macro nowcasting approach
that uses machine learning algorithms to directly exploit the full cross-section of accounting
information from 21, 061 publicly listed U.S. firms for nowcasting U.S. aggregate output.
Rather than relying on aggregated corporate indicators, the proposed framework preserves
the rich information contained in firm-level heterogeneity and cross-firm interactions that
is often lost through aggregation. The empirical results show that the proposed approach
significantly improves nowcasting accuracy, reducing the root mean squared error by more
than 70% relative to a random walk benchmark and substantially outperforming models
based on aggregated accounting data. Firm-level corporate accounting data also improve
nowcasting performance by about 17.35% relative to models using mixed-frequency aggregate
macroeconomic and financial predictors. Our feature-importance analysis shows that the
firms with larger assets, lower book-to-market ratios, or lower leverage, and the firms in

Introduction
Timely measurement of macroeconomic conditions is crucial for economic decision-making by
both policymakers and economic agents (e.g., Aruoba and Diebold 2010, Aruoba, Diebold,
and Scotti 2009).
In practice, however, key macroeconomic statistics are released with
substantial delays, particularly for low-frequency indicators such as gross domestic product
(GDP). These informational frictions highlight the distinction between forecasting and now-
casting. While forecasting targets future economic outcomes, nowcasting focuses on assessing
current conditions in real time, providing estimates of the present, the very near future, or
the recent past (Ba´nbura, Giannone, Modugno, and Reichlin 2013). By exploiting timely,
often higher-frequency information and accounting for the flow of data releases, macroeco-
nomic nowcasting helps bridge this information gap by producing early estimates of economic
activity before official data become available (e.g., Andreou, Ghysels, and Kourtellos 2013,
Antol´ın-D´ıaz, Drechsel, and Petrella 2024, Babii, Ghysels, and Striaukas 2022, Barnett,
Chauvet, and Leiva-Leon 2016, Clements and Galv˜ao 2008, 2009, Giannone, Reichlin, and
Small 2008, Wright 2019).
In recent years, accounting information of listed firms has been increasingly used in
macroeconomic forecasting and nowcasting because of its timeliness and close connection to
aggregate economic activity. Unlike traditional macroeconomic variables, which are often
subject to publication delays and revisions, corporate financial reports—released quarterly
and containing measures such as earnings, revenues, and returns on capital—provide timely
signals that reflect real-time conditions at the corporate level.
These signals are closely
linked to fluctuations in macroeconomic output. In a seminal study, Konchitchki and Pata-
toukas (2014a) show that aggregate accounting earnings contain predictive information for
2

future GDP growth, establishing a direct link between accounting information and macroe-
conomic activity, and Konchitchki and Patatoukas (2014b) provide evidence that accounting
profitability offers predictive content for GDP beyond that reflected in stock returns. Nal-
lareddy and Ogneva (2017) find that earnings dispersion adds incremental value in predicting
future GDP growth. Studies such as Kalay, Nallareddy, and Sadka (2018), Kim, Pandit, and
Wasley (2016), and Konchitchki, Luo, Ma, and Wu (2016) highlight the correlation between
accounting information and future macroeconomic shocks. Ball, Gallo, and Ghysels (2019)
show that smoother earnings improve GDP prediction, while Gaertner, Kausar, and Steele
(2020) report that negative earnings changes are more informative for future GDP than
positive changes. Abdalla and Carabias (2022) show that aggregate special items provide
timely signals for real GDP growth, and Abdalla, Carabias, and Patatoukas (2021) show that
sector-level accounting data enhance nowcasting accuracy within the canonical dynamic fac-
tor nowcasting framework of Giannone, Reichlin, and Small (2008). More recently, Gallo,
Jin, and Sridharan (2026) further examine the relationship between aggregate earnings and
GDP and investigate how aggregate accounting measures signal future economic conditions.
Collectively, these studies underscore the value of accounting data as a complementary source
of information, offering timely predictive signals that are not fully captured by conventional
macroeconomic or aggregate predictors.
In the existing literature, firm-level accounting information is typically aggregated into
higher-level measures before being used for macroeconomic forecasting or nowcasting. For
example, Konchitchki and Patatoukas (2014a,b) aggregate firm-level accounting variables
into value-weighted market-level averages.
Similarly, Abdalla, Carabias, and Patatoukas
(2021) construct sector-level aggregates and incorporate them into a dynamic factor model
(DFM ) for GDP nowcasting.
However, aggregation of firm-level accounting data inevitably leads to information loss
(Feige and Watts 1972, Orcutt, Watts, and Edwards 1968). Mechanically, these aggregation
approaches compress the cross-sectional distribution of firm outcomes into a low-dimensional
representation, eliminating firm heterogeneity, cross-firm interactions, nonlinear dynamics,
and sudden structural changes, thereby reducing the effective information available for es-
timation and prediction. This raises a key question at the heart of our study: how can
new real-time economic signals be learned from firm-level accounting data for macroeco-
nomic nowcasting, while preserving the richness of firm-level information and minimizing
the information loss inherent in conventional aggregation approaches?
To address this question, we propose a micro-to-macro nowcasting approach that learns
real-time economic signals from a large panel of firm-level accounting data covering all 21,061
3

publicly listed U.S. firms to nowcast the U.S. aggregate output. Unlike conventional ap-
proaches that aggregate firm-level variables into sector- or market-level measures or into
latent factors via factor extraction, the proposed approach operates directly on the large
firm-level panel, retaining the richness of micro-level accounting information. We employ
machine learning methods to exploit firm-level heterogeneity and interactions. Through reg-
ularization and related techniques, these methods can effectively handle high-dimensional
panels and capture both linear and nonlinear relationships, thereby minimizing the informa-
tion loss inherent in conventional aggregation approaches.
More specifically, we construct a predictive panel based on three firm-level accounting
variables that are widely used in the literature to forecast or nowcast macroeconomic ac-
tivity: return on net operating assets (RNOA), revenue growth (Growth), and accounting
earnings (Earnings). These measures provide informative signals of corporate activity while
capturing complementary dimensions of firm performance—profitability, growth, and oper-
ating outcomes. RNOA captures operating profitability and capital efficiency, measuring
how effectively firms generate income from invested capital and thus serving as a proxy for
supply-side productivity. Growth reflects expansion in sales and demand conditions, provid-
ing information about changes in output and market activity on the demand side. Earnings,
in turn, summarizes overall profit performance, thereby capturing broader corporate profit
conditions. Using these firm-level accounting measures for all publicly listed U.S. firms yields
a large predictive panel. We then apply a range of machine learning methods to this large
panel to nowcast U.S. real GDP. Our main findings are as follows:
First, our micro-to-macro nowcasting approach substantially improves the accuracy of
real GDP growth nowcasts relative to the random walk (RW ) benchmark, reducing RMSE by
more than 70% on average. Among alternative variable combinations, the pair of RNOA and
Earnings performs best, achieving the lowest average RMSE ratio of 0.287, while adding a
third accounting variable yields little further improvement. This suggests that, in accounting-
based nowcasting, more accounting variables are not necessarily better; a parsimonious choice
may be sufficient to capture the most informative dimensions of firm fundamentals.
In
addition, our method works well even early in the quarter, when more than 40% of firms
have yet to report accounting information for the previous quarter, and nowcasts are already
nearly as accurate as those at the end of the quarter. This highlights an important advantage
of the micro-to-macro approach, namely its ability to extract useful real-time macroeconomic
signals from limited firm-level information available early in the quarter. These gains remain
robust during periods of heightened economic uncertainty and volatility, including the 2008
global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.
4

Second, to quantify how much information is lost when firm-level accounting signals are
aggregated, we compare our micro-to-macro nowcasts with those based on aggregated ac-
counting data, including value-weighted market- or sector-level measures and dynamic factor
model (DFM )-extracted factors. Our approach delivers substantial gains over these alter-
natives across all variable combinations, highlighting the value of exploiting firm-level infor-
mation directly rather than through aggregation. Specifically, the micro-to-macro nowcasts
reduce RMSE by 25.13% over market-level aggregates, 15.21% over sector-level aggregates,
and 46.52% over the DFM -based factors. Moreover, among value-weighted accounting ag-
gregates, nowcasting performance improves as the level of aggregation decreases: sector-level
aggregates outperform market-level aggregates, while firm-level data perform best overall.
These results underscore a key insight of our paper: exploiting firm-level accounting data at
the micro level is crucial for improving nowcasting performance, as it preserves heterogeneity
across firms and interactions among them that are largely masked by aggregation.
Third, we assess whether firm-level accounting variables contain incremental information
for nowcasting over and above that captured by mixed-frequency macroeconomic and finan-
cial predictors. We use a macro-financial dataset that is widely used in the macroeconomic
nowcasting literature and provides broad coverage of U.S. economic conditions, spanning
real activity, consumption, labor markets, prices, housing and construction, external trade,
and financial conditions. Our results show that firm-level accounting data deliver superior
nowcasting performance, with a 15.99% reduction in RMSE over macroeconomic and fi-
nancial predictors. While combining the two information sets yields a 16.18% reduction, it
provides little improvement over accounting data alone, suggesting limited incremental value
of aggregate predictors once firm-level information is incorporated.
Lastly, we examine feature importance of individual firms in economic output nowcasting.
Firm-level importance is highly heterogeneous and varies over time, with limited overlap early
in the quarter and greater stability toward the end of the quarter. When firms are sorted
separately by characteristics, larger firms, firms with lower book-to-market ratios, and firms
with lower leverage account for a large share of total importance. At the sectoral level,
industrials, utilities, and consumer discretionary play especially important roles. Over time,
industrials dominate early in the sample, while utilities rise steadily after the global financial
crisis and become the leading sector by around 2013. These findings further highlight the
value of using firm-level accounting data, as the importance of individual firms varies across
firm characteristics, sectors, and over time in ways that are masked by aggregation, making
it harder to identify the firms most relevant for nowcasting.

Main Results
In this section, we present the empirical results to evaluate the effectiveness of our micro-
to-macro nowcasting approach. Specifically, we assess the extent to which incorporating
individual firm-level accounting data improves GDP nowcasting performance relative to the
benchmark model. We examine both overall (static) predictive accuracy and time-varying
performance, with particular attention to periods of macroeconomic instability such as re-
cessions, the global financial crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Examining performance across different information cutoff dates, we find no clear evi-
dence that RMSE ratios systematically decline as the quarter progresses. This highlights a
key advantage of the micro-to-macro approach: even at t = −8, when more than 40% of
firms have yet to report accounting information for the previous quarter, nowcasts based on
the available firm-level information perform nearly as well as those constructed at t = 0. This
suggests that much of the useful predictive content is already available early in the quarter.
A plausible explanation is that firms are exposed to common macroeconomic and industry
shocks and are connected through production and information networks. As a result, ac-
counting information released by early-reporting firms can also be informative about firms
that report later. Moreover, early reporters are not necessarily a random subset of firms.
They are often bellwether firms within their industries, and their disclosures may therefore
contain disproportionately important information about sectoral and aggregate economic
conditions (Foster 1981). This interpretation is consistent with the granular view of aggre-
gate fluctuations: in economies with fat-tailed firm-size distributions, the performance of
7

large firms can have non-negligible effects on aggregate activity (Carvalho and Gabaix 2013,
Gabaix 2011). It is also consistent with the network view that firm- and sector-level shocks
can propagate through input-output linkages, further amplifying the macroeconomic rele-
vance of information released by central or bellwether firms (Acemoglu et al. 2012). In this
sense, early disclosures may partly substitute for information that becomes available only
later, enabling the model to produce accurate nowcasts well before the end of the quarter,
even when reporting coverage remains incomplete.
Comparisons across specifications reveal several important patterns. Among the single-
variable models, RNOA delivers the lowest average RMSE ratio, followed closely by Growth,
with Earnings performing slightly worse. This suggests that operating profitability con-
tains somewhat stronger contemporaneous signals than revenue or earnings growth when
considered alone. The differences, however, are statistically small.
Moving from one-variable to two-variable specifications generally improves performance.
The combination of RNOA and Earnings performs best overall, achieving the lowest average
RMSE ratio (0.287), corresponding to about a 71% reduction over the RW benchmark.3
In contrast, adding the third accounting variable does not yield substantial additional
gains. The three-variable specification achieves an average RMSE ratio of 0.291, which is
slightly worse than the best two-variable model. This finding indicates that most of the
relevant macroeconomic information embedded in firm-level accounting data can already be
captured by a parsimonious combination of RNOA and Earnings, with limited incremental
benefit from further including Growth.
The strong nowcasting performance of RNOA-based models is economically intuitive.
RNOA captures operating profitability relative to invested capital and thus reflects firms’
production efficiency. At the aggregate level, GDP growth reflects value added from the uti-
lization of labor and capital, making RNOA a micro-level proxy for aggregate productivity.
Moreover, firm profits contribute to aggregate corporate profits, which constitute an impor-
tant component of economic output and income (Fischer and Merton 1984, Marcuss 2004),
implying that firm-level operating profitability provides timely signals of aggregate economic
performance. The predictive value of RNOA for aggregate output is also supported by prior
evidence (Abdalla et al. 2021, Konchitchki and Patatoukas 2014b).
The large predictive gains from combining RNOA and Earnings likely reflect the com-

Prior evidence suggests that the predictive power
of earnings for aggregate economic activity operates through several macroeconomic chan-

and Ogneva 2017). Taken together, these variables capture both operational efficiency and
broader income dynamics that are closely linked to aggregate economic output.
The limited incremental gain from adding Growth to the RNOA–Earnings specification
likely reflects both the nature of the information captured by revenue growth and its overlap
with the other two variables. Growth measures top-line revenue growth and therefore mainly
captures changes in demand conditions and other short-run fluctuations faced by firms,
consistent with prior studies that interpret firm-level sales dynamics in this way (e.g., Bloom
et al. 2007, Guiso and Parigi 1999, Love and Zicchino 2006). This helps explain why the
RNOA–Growth combination performs reasonably well.
However, compared with RNOA,
revenue growth is a less direct measure of economic value creation because it does not
account for operating costs or the capital employed to generate sales. Moreover, the marginal
contribution of Growth is limited because much of the economic information it contains is
already captured by RNOA and Earnings. Sales growth affects operating income and is
therefore reflected in RNOA through operating profitability, while its effects on net income—
after operating costs, financing expenses, taxes, and other adjustments—are captured by
Earnings. As a result, once RNOA and Earnings are jointly included, Growth adds little
incremental information for nowcasting GDP.

approach during high-volatility periods. The recession periods, as identified by the NBER,

periods of instability: the global financial crisis spanning from 2007:Q2 to 2010:Q2, and the

4.3
Robustness Check
We examine the robustness of our results by nowcasting nominal GDP growth. The micro-
to-macro models deliver substantial improvements over the RW benchmark, with RMSE
reductions of about 70%. We next consider real gross domestic income (GDI) growth, an
alternative measure of economic activity representing the income side of the BEA’s NIPA.
Prior research, such as Nalewaik (2010), highlights the value of GDI as a complementary
indicator to GDP. Our approach again delivers clear improvements in nowcasting accuracy
over the benchmark. Due to space limitations, all these results are reported in Appendix
C.3. Overall, the results are consistent with those for real GDP growth, supporting the
reliability and generalizability of the micro-to-macro approach.

Feature Importance
In this section, we examine the feature importance of firm-level accounting data for GDP
nowcasting. Specifically, we assess the heterogeneous contributions of individual firms to
GDP nowcasts, investigate how importance varies across firm characteristics, and explore
differences across industry sectors. This analysis underscores a key advantage of our micro-
to-macro approach over existing methods, especially those based on aggregation. Because our
approach retains firm-level information, it enables us to identify the firms most relevant for
GDP nowcasting and to examine the firm characteristics associated with their importance.
This, in turn, offers a new empirical insight into how heterogeneous firms contribute to
aggregate macroeconomic outcomes.

nowcasts for real GDP growth. All importance results are based on the RF model, which
emerges as the top-performing algorithm under this accounting-variable specification.5

Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a micro-to-macro approach that uses a large panel of firm-level
accounting data to nowcast aggregate economic output. Our results show that the micro-
to-macro approach substantially improves nowcasting accuracy for real GDP growth upon
the RW benchmark, reducing RMSE by more than 70% on average and maintaining strong
performance during periods of heightened economic uncertainty. Among alternative account-
ing variable combinations, the pair of RNOA and Earnings performs best, while adding a
third accounting variable (Growth) yields little further improvement. The micro-to-macro
nowcasting approach performs well early in the quarter, indicating that useful macroeco-
nomic signals can already be extracted from limited firm-level information before reporting
coverage becomes complete.
The superior performance over the use of aggregated accounting predictors further high-
lights the advantage of the micro-to-macro approach in preserving firm-level heterogeneity
and cross-firm interactions that are typically lost in aggregation. Moreover, compared with
mixed-frequency macroeconomic and financial predictors, firm-level accounting data already
contain rich and timely signals of aggregate economic activity, while combining the two
information sets yields little additional gains.
The feature-importance analysis reveals substantial heterogeneity in firm-level contribu-
tions to GDP nowcasting across both firms and information cutoff dates. Firms with larger
assets, lower book-to-market ratios, or lower leverage contribute more to GDP nowcasts. At
the sector level, firms in industrials, utilities, and consumer discretionary play particularly
important roles. These findings highlight an important advantage of the micro-to-macro
approach: it not only improves GDP nowcasting accuracy but also offers insights into which
firms and sectors contain predictive information.
Overall, our study shows that firm-level accounting data contain rich macroeconomic
information that can be effectively extracted without prior aggregation. By bridging micro-
level corporate behavior and macroeconomic outcomes, our micro-to-macro approach offers a
novel perspective on real-time economic measurement and a useful tool for assessing current
economic conditions.
